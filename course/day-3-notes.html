    Finding Limits Algebraically.

    Since finding limits using graphs depends on having a good (well-labeled and detailed) graph, graphs are generally best suited for estimating limits. But can you tell the difference between a limit of \(5\) or \(5.0000001\text{?}\) Often, we want a way to show that the limit we’ve found is exactly correct. As usual, the answer lies in algebra!
    🔗
    Step 1: Direct Substitution — try plugging in the value that \(x\) is approaching.
    🔗
    Often (especially for common functions), we can find a limit simply by substituting \(x=a\) in our function. This method is effective for functions that are continuous at the point of interest.
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint.

    Notation Warning.

    There are some notational things to be careful of with limits. In short, make sure every step of your work has both a limit operator (\(\lim_{x \to a}\)) and an argument (\(f(x)\)) until you’ve successfully evaluated the limit. That is, the variables and the operator should always disappear in the same step.
    🔗
    In many of the problems we’ll do this semester, some combination or variation of these techniques will be sufficient to find limits algebraically. But it takes lots of practice, and there are a few weird cases to consider.
